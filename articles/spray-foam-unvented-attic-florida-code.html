Spray Foam Unvented Attics Under Florida Building Code: R806.5 Insulation and HVAC Protection

A technical evaluation of Florida Building Code Section R806.5 unvented roof assemblies, open-cell vs closed-cell foam, and attic duct sweating elimination.

By SoutheastAEC™ Building Science • Published October 2026 • Florida Building Code (FBC) Residential Section R806.5 / ASTM C1029 / FBC Section R316
Quick Summary

In the subtropical climate of Florida and the Gulf Coast, traditional vented attics routinely reach extreme temperatures between 135°F and 145°F during summer months. When air conditioning ductwork and air handlers are located within this superheated, humid space, conductive thermal losses reduce system cooling capacity by 25% to 35%, and cold metal duct surfaces frequently drop below the ambient attic dew point, producing severe duct sweating and ceiling drywall damage. Converting the attic into an unvented, conditioned space under Florida Building Code (FBC) Residential Section R806.5 moves the building's thermal and air boundary from the flat ceiling plane up to the underside of the roof sheathing. Applying spray polyurethane foam directly to the roof deck eliminates attic heat gain, protects mechanical equipment, and slashes whole-home cooling energy consumption.

  • The Duct Condensation Penalty: Operating chilled 55°F air conditioning ducts inside a 140°F vented attic with a 78°F dew point causes liquid condensation to soak into ceiling insulation and drywall.
  • FBC Section R806.5 Code Compliance: Unvented attic assemblies legally require sealing all soffit, ridge, and gable vents, eliminating interior ceiling vapor retarders, and providing direct roof-deck insulation.
  • Open-Cell vs Closed-Cell Physics: In Climate Zone 2A, open-cell foam (0.5 lb/cu.ft) requires a vapor-retarding coating (Class II vapor retarder) to prevent inward vapor condensation against roof sheathing, whereas closed-cell foam (2.0 lb/cu.ft) serves as its own impermeable vapor barrier.
  • Shingle Temperature Impact Truth: Empirical field studies by the Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC) confirm that roof deck spray foam increases asphalt shingle temperatures by only 2°F to 5°F, refuting warranty voidance myths.

The Vented Attic Thermal Penalty in Subtropical Climates

Traditional residential architecture in Florida historically relied on open soffit vents and ridge vents to exhaust hot air from attic cavities. In a hot-dry climate, this passive convective ventilation functions adequately.

In the humid Southeast, however, outdoor air entering through soffit vents carries an extreme latent moisture load. When this humid air encounters dark asphalt shingles or concrete tiles absorbing intense solar radiation, attic air temperatures soar to 140°F.

A typical split-system air conditioner located in an unconditioned attic must circulate 55°F conditioned supply air through sheet metal or flexible ductwork suspended in this 140°F furnace. The temperature differential ($\Delta T$) across the thin R-6 or R-8 duct insulation exceeds 85°F.

This massive thermal gradient forces the air conditioning system to expend more than a third of its total cooling capacity merely fighting conductive heat gain through the ducts before the air ever reaches a bedroom.

When duct connections leak slightly or insulation settles, the cold metal duct boots chill down below the humid attic dew point (typically 76°F to 80°F). Moisture condenses rapidly on the outer duct jackets, dripping gallons of water onto the gypsum ceiling below and ruining living room ceilings.

Florida Building Code Section R806.5 Mandates

To eliminate this mechanical penalty, the Florida Building Code (FBC) Residential Section R806.5 provides the statutory framework for designing and permitting unvented attic assemblies.

The code strictly prohibits partial measures; an unvented attic is an all-or-nothing building science system governed by four prescriptive requirements:

1
Complete Vent Elimination: The unvented attic space must be completely isolated from the outdoors. All existing eave vents, continuous soffit vents, ridge vents, off-ridge vents, and gable louvers must be permanently sealed using rigid air barriers and expanding foam sealant.
2
Elimination of Ceiling Vapor Retarders: No vapor retarder (such as polyethylene plastic or kraft paper facing) may be installed on the ceiling plane between the living space and the attic. The attic and living space must form a shared hygrothermal zone.
3
Direct Roof Deck Insulation: Thermal insulation must be applied in direct contact with the underside of the structural roof sheathing. Spray polyurethane foam must completely coat all roof rafters, truss top chords, and sheathing seams to prevent air leakage.
4
Moisture Vapor Management: In Climate Zones 1A, 2A, and 3A, the insulation assembly must satisfy strict water vapor permeance rules to prevent inward moisture accumulation on the structural roof deck.

Open-Cell vs Closed-Cell Polyurethane: Hygrothermal Dynamics

When selecting spray foam for Florida roof decks, architects and contractors must choose between two fundamentally different chemical formulations: low-density open-cell foam and medium-density closed-cell foam.

Open-cell spray polyurethane foam (ocSPF) exhibits a nominal density of 0.5 pounds per cubic foot ($0.5\text{ lb/ft}^3$) and delivers an R-value of approximately R-3.6 to R-3.8 per inch. Its microscopic cellular matrix consists of interconnected open cells that allow water vapor to pass through relatively easily, exhibiting a high vapor permeance of 15 to 30 perms at a 3-inch thickness.

Because open-cell foam is vapor permeable, high outdoor humidity during sunny days can drive water vapor through roof sheathing joints into the foam. In Climate Zone 2A (Central and South Florida), FBC Section R806.5 requires that when open-cell foam is used, an approved Class II vapor retarder coating (such as a vapor-retarding latex paint with a perm rating between 0.1 and 1.0 perms) must be sprayed across the exposed interior surface of the foam.

Closed-cell spray polyurethane foam (ccSPF) possesses a nominal density of 2.0 pounds per cubic foot ($2.0\text{ lb/ft}^3$) and delivers an exceptional thermal resistance of R-6.5 to R-7.0 per inch. The closed, gas-filled cellular structure forms an impermeable air barrier and, at a thickness of 1.5 inches or greater, serves as an approved Class II vapor retarder (less than 1.0 perm under ASTM E96).

In high-risk hurricane zones, closed-cell foam offers a massive structural co-benefit. Testing conducted by the University of Florida confirms that applying a 3-inch layer of 2.0-lb closed-cell foam between roof rafters glues the plywood roof sheathing to the trusses, increasing wind uplift resistance by more than 250% and preventing sheathing pull-through during Category 4 windstorms.

Thermal Impacts on Shingles and Tile: Disproving the Warranty Myth

A common misconception among homeowners is that eliminating attic ventilation causes asphalt shingles to "bake" and curl under extreme heat, prematurely destroying the roof and voiding manufacturer warranties.

Extensive empirical research conducted by the Florida Solar Energy Center (FSEC) and the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has thoroughly disproved this concern. Solar radiation striking the exterior face of an asphalt shingle is primarily dissipated through outward radiant re-emission to the sky and convective heat shedding to the outdoor breeze.

The downward conductive loss into the attic represents less than 5% of the total solar heat dissipation mechanism. FSEC field monitoring of identical homes demonstrated that installing spray foam on the underside of roof sheathing increases the peak shingle temperature by only 2°F to 5°F on a 95°F afternoon (e.g., from 162°F up to 166°F).

Major shingle manufacturers, including GAF, Owens Corning, and CertainTeed, officially recognize FBC Section R806.5 unvented attics in their technical warranty bulletins. As long as the unvented roof assembly complies with building code requirements, standard material defect warranties remain fully intact.

Thermal Barrier Fire Coatings: FBC Section R316

Spray polyurethane foam is a petroleum-based cellular plastic that presents serious combustion hazards if exposed to open flames. The Florida Building Code Section R316 strictly mandates that all foam plastic insulation must be protected from the interior of the building by an approved thermal barrier.

In standard living spaces, 1/2-inch gypsum drywall serves as the prescriptive 15-minute thermal barrier. In an unvented attic accessed only for mechanical servicing, Section R316.5.3 permits an alternative ignition barrier.

Approved ignition barrier options include applying an engineered intumescent fire-protective coating sprayed directly over the foam surface. When exposed to high heat or flame, intumescent coatings swell into an insulating carbonaceous foam char that prevents flame propagation and suppresses smoke generation under ASTM E84 testing standards.

Empower First: Evaluating Attic Temperature and Duct Leakage

Homeowners can evaluate whether their vented attic is creating a severe thermal and mechanical penalty using basic diagnostic checks:

  • Measure the Midday Attic Temperature Differential: Place an accurate remote thermometer in the center of your attic space during a 90°F afternoon. If the temperature exceeds 125°F while your air conditioner runs continuously, your ducts are operating in an extreme loss environment.
  • Inspect Duct Outer Jacket Condensation: Climb into the attic and feel the underside of the flexible duct runs and metal plenum boxes. If the outer silver vapor barrier is damp, slick with water, or showing brown rust rings on sheet metal screws, active duct sweating is occurring.
  • Look for Ceiling Drywall Water Staining: Examine ceilings beneath attic duct pathways for yellow or faint brown ring stains. These stains are frequently misdiagnosed as roof leaks when they are actually caused by duct condensation pooling on top of the drywall.
  • Check Ridge and Soffit Vent Condition: Look up at your roof line from the yard to confirm that existing ridge vents and soffit grilles are clear of debris, insect nests, or dislodged insulation baffles.

Safety Net Second: When to Engage a Licensed Spray Foam Contractor

Spray foam insulation requires chemical handling expertise, specialized proportioner rigs, and rigorous building code compliance verification:

  • Two-Component Chemical Ratio and Temperature Control: Spray polyurethane foam is formed on-site by mixing equal parts of Isocyanate (A-side) and Polyol resin (B-side) at high pressure (1,200 to 1,500 PSI) and elevated temperature (125°F to 140°F). If a poorly maintained rig dispenses an off-ratio chemical mix, the foam can fail to cure, creating a spongy mess that emits lingering off-gassing odors and requires complete mechanical removal.
  • Atmospheric Combustion Equipment Lockout: You cannot spray foam an unvented attic that contains an atmospheric gas furnace or gas water heater that draws combustion air from the attic cavity. A licensed mechanical contractor must convert appliances to direct-vent sealed combustion units or pipe dedicated outdoor air directly to the burners to avoid deadly carbon monoxide poisoning.
  • Intumescent Fire Coating Certification: Applying intumescent fire coatings requires certified wet-film thickness gauges to confirm the coating meets the manufacturer's tested mil thickness. A licensed insulation contractor must provide building code officials with a signed Certificate of Completion detailing foam density, thickness, and fire barrier compliance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does spray foam in the attic void shingle warranties in Florida?

No, major roofing manufacturers (including GAF, Owens Corning, and CertainTeed) explicitly permit spray foam applied directly to the underside of roof sheathing under modern building codes. Rigorous thermal studies confirm that spray foam increases shingle temperatures by only 2°F to 5°F, which has no measurable impact on shingle longevity.

Is open-cell or closed-cell spray foam better for Florida attics?

Closed-cell spray foam is generally superior for coastal Florida homes. Closed-cell foam (2.0 lb density) provides an R-value of R-6.8 per inch, acts as a complete water vapor barrier without requiring extra coatings, sheds bulk water if a tile breaks, and increases roof sheathing wind uplift resistance by over 250% during hurricanes.

Do I need to condition the attic after spray foaming the roof deck?

Yes, under modern building science practice, an unvented attic must be treated as semi-conditioned space. Contractors typically introduce a small amount of conditioned air (approximately 50 CFM of supply air per 1,000 square feet) or install a dehumidifier return duct to maintain relative humidity in the attic below 55% and prevent moisture stagnation.

How much does it cost to spray foam an unvented attic in Florida?

Converting a standard 2,000-square-foot Florida attic to an unvented assembly typically costs between $4,500 and $7,500 for open-cell foam, and between $7,000 and $11,500 for closed-cell foam. Most homeowners recoup this investment through 20% to 35% reductions in monthly cooling electricity consumption over 5 to 8 years.
