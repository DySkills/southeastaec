How to Retrofit Hurricane Straps and Rafter Tie-Downs: Continuous Load Path Engineering

A practical jobsite guide to installing Simpson Strong-Tie HGA10, H10A, and masonry straps to anchor wood trusses against ASCE 7 wind uplift.

By SoutheastAEC™ Building Science • Published October 2026 • Florida Building Code (FBC) Existing Building / ASCE 7-22 / ASTM A653
Quick Summary

Hurricane wind damage in the Southeast frequently begins with the separation of the roof structure from the exterior walls. Prior to modern building code updates following Hurricane Andrew in 1992 and the 2002 Florida Building Code, millions of Southern homes were framed using simple toenailing, which provides less than 350 pounds of uplift resistance per truss. Retrofitting engineered structural hurricane clips establishes a continuous mechanical load path that transfers aerodynamic suction forces safely from the roof sheathing down into the wall framing and concrete foundation.

  • Continuous Mechanical Path Mandate: Uplift forces must transfer uninterrupted from roof sheathing through trusses, wall studs, and sole plates down into concrete footings.
  • Fastener Shear vs Toenail Splitting: Three 8d toenails fail catastrophically under cyclic hurricane loads, whereas engineered steel straps provide over 1,250 pounds of tested uplift capacity.
  • Masonry vs Wood-Frame Retrofits: Concrete block tie-downs require concrete screw anchors into solid grouted bond beams, while wood framing utilizes structural screws driven into double top plates.
  • Wind Mitigation Insurance Premium Reductions: Installing verified hurricane straps qualifying under Florida OIR-B1-1802 inspection forms frequently reduces annual homeowners insurance premiums by 30% to 55%.

Jobsite Requisites, Connectors, and Fastener Schedules

Retrofitting hurricane hardware in confined attic spaces or through exterior soffits requires specialized structural connectors and heavy-gauge fasteners. Never substitute standard drywall screws or unrated framing nails for engineer-specified structural fasteners.

Critical Fastener Substitution Warning

Standard bugle-head drywall screws and gold construction deck screws possess brittle, heat-treated carbon steel cores. Under cyclic hurricane wind shear and uplift vibration, brittle screws snap cleanly at the head. You must only install manufacturer-certified structural screws or hot-dip galvanized nails matching connector documentation.

Step-by-Step Hurricane Strap Retrofit Protocol

Step 1: Attic Access Preparation and Truss Inspection

Clear a stable pathway across the attic joists using 3/4-inch plywood crawlboards placed across ceiling joists. Stepping directly onto gypsum drywall ceilings will immediately cause catastrophic ceiling blowouts and severe physical injury.

Examine the existing truss-to-wall connections along the perimeter exterior walls using a high-output inspection light. Document whether the existing framing relies on toe-nailed connections, single-wrap clips with insufficient nails, or embedded straps that were cut short by original framing crews.

Measure the clearance between the roof sheathing, truss heels, and the wall top plate to determine which connector profile fits your clearance envelope. If roof pitch is low (4:12 or less), interior attic access may be too tight to swing a hammer, necessitating right-angle structural screw drivers or exterior soffit-access retrofitting.

Step 2: Surface Preparation and Framing Clearance

Clear all loose fiberglass or blown cellulose insulation away from the perimeter wall plate intersection using a shop vacuum or dustpan. The wood mating surfaces between the truss chord and top plate must be completely bare to ensure tight metal-to-wood contact.

Check for structural wood splitting or decay around existing toe-nails using a probe. If the bottom chord of the truss is split from historic nailing, you must install an engineered wood scab (a 2x4 sister block secured with structural screws) to restore the load-bearing substrate.

Verify that the double top plate is securely fastened together without gaps or separation. If the upper plate has pulled away from the lower plate, drive two 3-1/2-inch structural wood screws through both plates to draw them firmly together before mounting clips.

Step 3: Installing Wood-to-Wood Connectors (H10A / HGA10)

Position the Simpson Strong-Tie H10A hurricane tie-down clip against the side of the roof truss, allowing the upper strap to fold over the top edge of the truss chord. The lower flange must sit flush against the wide face of the double top plate.

Drive manufacturer-specified Simpson Strong-Drive SD structural screws through every prepunched hole in the upper connector strap into the truss chord. Ensure screws enter perpendicular to the wood grain to avoid tear-out along the lumber edge.

Secure the lower flange to the double top plate by driving structural screws into all designated plate holes. Code requires that fasteners penetrate through the upper plate and anchor firmly into the lower top plate to prevent inter-plate delamination under severe uplift tension.

Step 4: Installing Masonry-to-Wood Retrofit Clips

In concrete block stucco (CBS) construction, the wood truss rests on a 2x6 pressure-treated wood buck anchored into a poured concrete bond beam. Position a heavy-gauge angle connector (such as the Simpson HGA10 or FGTR) tight into the intersection of the truss and the concrete beam.

Pre-drill pilot holes into the concrete bond beam using a rotary hammer drill equipped with a carbide-tipped masonry bit matching your anchor diameter. Verify that the hole depth exceeds the anchor length by at least 1/2 inch to accommodate concrete dust accumulation.

Blow all residual concrete dust from the drill holes using compressed air or a hand bulb pump. Drive high-strength concrete screw anchors through the bracket into the poured concrete bond beam, then secure the top bracket flange into the wood truss using structural wood screws.

Step 5: Verification, Stamping, and Insulation Restoration

Inspect every installed connector along the entire perimeter to confirm that zero fastener holes were skipped. An empty nail hole in an engineered connector reduces the ultimate structural load capacity of that connection by 15% to 25%.

Photograph each connection clearly alongside a tape measure and a jobsite identification board showing your address. These photographs provide essential physical verification for municipal building inspectors and insurance wind mitigation certification forms (Uniform Mitigation Verification Inspection Form OIR-B1-1802).

Rake the blown insulation back over the exterior wall plates, taking care to install rigid baffle vents between each rafter bay. Baffles ensure that attic ventilation air from soffits flows cleanly over the insulation without causing wind washing or ceiling condensation.

Empower First: Homeowner Wind Mitigation Verification

Homeowners can document their structural tie-down status and determine whether an insurance discount applies before hiring a licensed contractor:

  • Check Current Insurance Mitigation Reports: Review Section 4 ("Roof-to-Wall Attachment") on your existing home wind mitigation inspection report. If the box is marked "Toenail" (defined as fewer than three nails per strap or plain toenails), you are missing out on substantial wind insurance credits.
  • Inspect Strap Nail Counts: Climb into the attic access hatch and count the exposed nail heads in three accessible roof-to-wall connectors. To qualify for the lucrative "Clip" rating under Florida insurance rules, a metal connector must have a minimum of three nails into the truss and two nails into the top plate.
  • Identify Strap Wraps: Check whether the metal strap wraps completely over the top of the truss chord or merely attaches to the side. Connectors that wrap completely over the top chord qualify for the highest "Single Wrap" or "Double Wrap" insurance classification.

Safety Net Second: When to Engage a Licensed Contractor

While installing clips along accessible interior attic perimeters is achievable for advanced DIYers, severe structural limitations require licensed engineering and contracting professionals:

  • Zero-Clearance Low-Pitch Roofs: On roofs with low pitches (3:12 or less), the vertical space at the exterior eave often drops below 10 inches, making interior fastening impossible. A licensed roofing or framing contractor must access the framing from the exterior by removing the fascia boards, gutter systems, and soffits.
  • Unreinforced Hollow CMU Walls: In older Florida homes built between 1950 and 1975, the top course of concrete blocks may be hollow rather than filled with a solid poured bond beam. Fastening anchors into hollow masonry provides zero uplift capacity; a licensed engineer must design through-bolt threaded rod assemblies anchored deep into tie-columns.
  • Gable-End Wall Bracing Retrofits: Gable end walls experience extreme lateral pressure during hurricanes, frequently blowing inward and causing total roof collapse. Retrofitting continuous L-brackets, horizontal tension ties, and vertical stud bracing requires an engineer-stamped structural design.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between hurricane clips and hurricane straps?

Hurricane clips are rigid steel brackets that attach to the side of a truss and connect to the wall top plate, resisting upward and lateral loads. Hurricane straps are longer, flexible galvanized steel bands that wrap completely over the top edge of the rafter or truss chord, providing superior uplift capacity exceeding 1,200 pounds per connection.

How much do hurricane straps reduce homeowners insurance in the Southeast?

In coastal zones across Florida, Georgia, and the Carolinas, upgrading from toenails to code-compliant hurricane clips or single-wrap straps typically reduces the windstorm portion of your homeowners insurance premium by 25% to 55%. For many coastal property owners, this translates to annual savings between $800 and $2,500.

Can hurricane straps be installed from the outside through the soffit?

Yes, on homes with low-slope roofs where interior attic clearance is less than 12 inches, exterior installation is the industry standard. Contractors remove the exterior soffit panels, install the hurricane straps through the exposed eave cavity, and reinstall or upgrade the soffits with impact-resistant aluminum or vinyl vented panels.

Are structural screws allowed instead of nails for hurricane tie-downs?

Yes, provided you use structural screws that are explicitly tested and certified by the connector manufacturer (such as Simpson Strong-Drive SD screws). Standard framing nails or structural screws are approved, but you must never use standard drywall or general-purpose deck screws, which shear easily under hurricane loads.
