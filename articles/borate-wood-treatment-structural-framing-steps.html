How to Apply Borate Wood Treatments to Structural Framing: Jobsite Protocol

A field contractor guide to mixing, spraying, and verifying disodium octaborate tetrahydrate penetration into crawlspace timbers and wall studs.

By SoutheastAEC™ Building Science • Published October 2026 • AWPA Standard M2 / EPA Reg. No. 64405-1 / OSHA 1910.1200
Quick Summary

Applying a field-delivered disodium octaborate tetrahydrate (DOT) treatment to exposed structural framing provides permanent, code-recognized immunity against subterranean termites and wood-decay fungi. During residential new construction, major renovations, or post-rot crawlspace restorations, the ideal window for treatment occurs when framing is fully assembled but prior to the installation of insulation and drywall. This jobsite protocol details the precise chemical mixing ratios, temperature parameters, spray pressure settings, and chemical indicator verification tests required to achieve deep cellular borate diffusion under American Wood Protection Association (AWPA) Standard M2.

  • Chemical Formulation Mechanics: Specifying glycol-enhanced disodium octaborate tetrahydrate (DOT) accelerates osmotic penetration deep into dense heartwood compared to basic surface salt sprays.
  • Water Temperature Dissolution: Mixing concentrated DOT powder requires warm water between 120°F and 140°F to ensure complete mineral dissolution and prevent sprayer nozzle clogging.
  • Two-Coat Wet-on-Wet Standard: Applying two sequential coats with a 20- to 30-minute absorption window guarantees the required 1.0 gallon per 200 square feet coverage rate.
  • Curcumin Indicator Verification: Spraying an alcoholic curcumin and salicylic acid reagent onto a core wood sample turns bright red to confirm successful borate penetration.

Chemical Ingestion and Runoff Caution

While disodium octaborate tetrahydrate exhibits low mammalian toxicity comparable to table salt, concentrated solutions must never be swallowed or inhaled in aerosolized spray mist. Take strict precautions to prevent liquid runoff into open waterways, storm drains, or agricultural soil, as high boron concentrations inhibit plant root development.

Step-by-Step Borate Application Protocol

Step 1: Substrate Preparation and Moisture Verification

Inspect all bare structural framing, floor joists, subflooring, and mudsills to confirm the wood surface is completely bare. Borate solutions rely on natural capillary osmosis through open wood pores; they cannot penetrate lumber coated with wax, oil, paint, or heavy sawdust accumulation.

Clean all framing surfaces using a commercial shop vacuum or a stiff wire brush to remove loose soil, dirt dauber nests, and mold residue. If framing has suffered surface mold, spray the lumber with an approved quaternary ammonium antimicrobial wash and allow it to dry before applying borates.

Measure the wood moisture content across multiple framing members using a calibrated pin-type moisture meter. Ideal wood moisture content for borate diffusion ranges between 12% and 18%.

If the wood is bone-dry (under 10% moisture content), the cellular diffusion process slows down significantly. Lightly misting the wood with clean warm water 15 minutes prior to chemical application re-opens surface capillaries and accelerates chemical penetration.

Step 2: Formulating the 1:1 Chemical Solution

Prepare your chemical batch in a heavy-duty 5-gallon polyethylene mixing bucket. If using an industrial glycol-based liquid formulation (such as Bora-Care), mix the concentrate with clean water at a strict 1:1 ratio by volume for active termite prevention.

Always use warm to hot water between 120°F and 140°F when preparing the mixture. Cold water increases the viscosity of the glycol concentrate, making it difficult to stir and causing thick clogs inside the spray equipment.

Pour the hot water into the bucket first, then slowly pour the thick borate concentrate into the water while running a heavy-duty drill equipped with a spiral mixing paddle. Blend the solution continuously for at least three to five minutes until the liquid transforms into a completely uniform, clear golden fluid with zero cloudiness or sediment.

Step 3: Calibrating the Chemical Sprayer

Pour the mixed borate solution into your commercial compression sprayer through a fine mesh strainer to catch any stray debris. Pressurize the tank to a steady working pressure of 20 to 30 PSI.

Select a brass or stainless steel flat-fan spray nozzle with an 80-degree spray angle (such as a TeeJet 8003 or 8004 fan tip). Avoid high-pressure, cone-shaped misting nozzles that atomize the chemical into airborne fog; you require a heavy, coarse droplet spray that thoroughly wets the wood surface without drifting.

Test the spray pattern against a piece of scrap cardboard from a distance of 12 to 18 inches. The spray should deliver a continuous, wet 12-inch horizontal band without streaking or sputtering.

Step 4: Applying the First Wetting Coat

Begin spraying at the base of the framing assembly, moving the spray wand in smooth, horizontal overlapping passes. Coat the bottom 24 inches of all exterior-facing studs, interior partition base plates, and the bottom faces of all subflooring and floor joists.

Hold the spray nozzle approximately 12 inches from the timber face, applying sufficient fluid until the wood surface appears completely saturated and begins to glisten. Ensure all structural end grain, fastener penetration holes, and lap joints receive heavy chemical saturation, as end grain absorbs fluid four times faster than side grain.

Monitor your chemical consumption rate closely to ensure adequate application density. A properly calibrated single pass covers approximately 400 square feet of wood surface per gallon of mixed solution.

Step 5: Applying the Second Coat and Verifying Penetration

Allow the treated framing to rest for 20 to 30 minutes following the first pass. During this absorption window, the initial liquid layer soaks deep into the wood pores, and the wet glossy sheen fades to a dull, matte appearance.

Apply a second continuous wetting coat over the exact same structural footprint, moving in the opposite direction. Applying this second pass while the wood remains damp (wet-on-wet) dramatically enhances osmotic capillary draw, driving the total chemical application density to the required target of one gallon per 200 square feet.

Allow the treated structure to dry and diffuse for at least 48 hours before installing fiberglass batt insulation, spray foam, or gypsum wallboard. Enclosing damp, freshly treated lumber traps surface moisture, which can cause drywall fastener corrosion and impede full chemical curing.

Step 6: Chemical Indicator Verification with Curcumin Reagent

To verify that the borate treatment has achieved adequate depth of penetration into the framing, extract an incremental wood core sample from a representative floor joist using a timber borer or increment hammer.

Place the core sample on a clean white testing tray. Drop several droplets of Reagent A (an alcoholic curcumin extract) onto the exposed wood fibers, followed immediately by Reagent B (a 10% hydrochloric and salicylic acid solution).

Observe the chemical color transformation on the wood sample over the next 60 seconds. Untreated wood retains a faint yellow or pale amber tint; wood containing active borate salts above 0.2% concentration transforms instantly into a vivid, deep crimson red.

Measure the depth of the crimson color band from the outer edge inward with a precision millimeter ruler. A successful professional application displays deep crimson penetration extending at least 1/4 inch into dense Douglas fir or southern yellow pine, and complete through-and-through penetration across 1-inch dimensional framing.

Empower First: Homeowner Framing Treatment Audit

Homeowners remodeling or building a new home can inspect the quality of the on-site borate application using simple visual and chemical criteria:

  • Check Manufacturer Coloring Dye Tint: Most commercial borate concentrates include an inert blue or red tracer dye. Walk the framed structure and verify that the bottom two feet of every single wall stud and sole plate displays an even, continuous pastel tint without missed skips or dry spots.
  • Inspect End Grain and Mudsill Penetrations: Check where floor joists rest on top of perimeter masonry sills; these structural junctions must be thoroughly saturated, as termites prefer attacking end grain where wood pores are naturally open.
  • Verify In-Wall Moisture Elimination Before Enclosure: Use a pin-type moisture meter to confirm that framing moisture has dropped below 15% before drywall crews begin hanging wallboard.
  • Request Official Treatment Documentation: Require your pest management contractor or builder to provide an official Certificate of Treatment detailing the EPA product registration number, total chemical gallons applied, and calculated square footage coverage.

Safety Net Second: When to Engage a Licensed Pest Control Specialist

While small-scale crawlspace spot treatments can be performed by experienced DIYers, comprehensive whole-home structural termite treatments require licensed professionals:

  • Whole-House Pre-Drywall Termite Warranty Certification: In many Southern states (including Florida, Georgia, and Louisiana), mortgage lenders require an official state-mandated wood-destroying organism (WDO) pre-treatment certificate (such as the Florida DACS 13645 form). Only a state-licensed commercial pest control operator can legally issue this binding structural warranty.
  • High-Hazard Crawlspace Fungus and Wood Shoring: If crawlspace timbers are already infected with active wood-decay fungi (Poria incrassata or water rot), merely spraying surface borates will not restore structural capacity. A licensed structural contractor must engineer shoring towers and sister replacement framing before chemical treatment proceeds.
  • Commercial Power-Spraying Rig Operations: Treating large multi-story timber buildings requires commercial high-output diaphragm pumps, dual-line heated chemical proportioners, and extension booms that exceed standard hand-pump sprayer capacity.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you apply borate treatment to painted or stained wood?

No, borate treatments must only be applied to completely bare, unfinished wood surfaces. Paints, sealers, polyurethane, and heavy stains seal the surface pores of the lumber, preventing the water-soluble borate mineral salts from diffusing into the cellular structure of the timber.

What is the mixing ratio for Bora-Care to treat termites?

For preventative and remedial treatment against subterranean and Formosan termites, commercial glycol-enhanced borate concentrate (such as Bora-Care) must be mixed with hot water at a strict 1:1 ratio by volume. A 1:1 ratio ensures the chemical concentration reaches the required 0.28 pounds per cubic foot boric oxide equivalent retention inside the wood.

How high up the wall studs do you need to spray borate?

Under standard building-code pest management specifications, spray application must cover the bottom 24 inches of all exterior-facing studs, interior partition studs, and bottom sole plates. In high-risk Formosan termite zones, many structural engineers specify treating all wall studs up to 48 inches or treating 100% of the home's wood framing package.

Does borate treatment wash away if it rains on the framing?

Borate mineral salts are water-soluble; if framing is subjected to continuous torrential downpours before the house is dried-in with a roof and housewrap, surface chemical can slowly leach out. However, if the chemical had 24 to 48 hours to diffuse deep into the wood fibers, brief rain showers will not remove the internal core treatment.
